# Supplier position notes — Jay Ambey Foods Products

**What this is.** A documentary and technical preparation pack for the goods-supplier side
of the Atal Awasiya Vidyalaya matter, built strictly from the records in this repository.

**What this is not.** Legal advice. Statutory references are indicative and must be
confirmed by counsel before they are relied on. Engage a food-law practitioner and
criminal-side counsel in Uttar Pradesh.

See [`case_context.md`](case_context.md) for the incident and FIR, and
[`defects_register.md`](defects_register.md) for the defects in the laboratory evidence.

## 1. Where you actually stand in the record

Four things in the documents work in your favour, and they should be established early.

### You are not in the accused list
FIR 0158/2026 arrays three persons: the manager of the housekeeping contractor, the
manager of M/s Shalu Associates, and a supervisor. **Jay Ambey Foods appears only in the
narrative**, described as a **सहयोगी (associate) firm**, with Shri Subhash Gupta named as its
manager. That is a materially different position from being an accused, and it should not be
allowed to drift.

### Your licence was valid and in scope
FSSAI State Licence **12719013000109**, Jhansi, *Food Services – Caterer*, issued
09-11-2024, **valid to 31-12-2026** — so valid on 16/07/2026. Renewals run continuously from
2019 with no gap. Product categories include 01 Dairy, **02 Fats and oils**, 06 Cereals and
16 Prepared Foods, so the articles in question fall inside your licensed scope.

No "unlicensed operation" allegation arises against you from this document.

### The cause of the illness is not established, and cannot now be
There is **no microbiological testing anywhere** in the eight analyst reports — no plate
counts, coliforms, *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *S. aureus* or *B. cereus* — and **no water
sample**, despite the caterer's own remedial step being the installation of an RO unit. All
samples were destroyed after certification.

The FIR pleads **BNS 271 (negligent act likely to spread infection)** and **BNS 275 (sale of
noxious food)**. Both require something the laboratory evidence does not contain. Nothing in
the reports establishes that any article was *noxious* or *unfit for consumption*, as against
merely below a quality standard.

### "Substandard" is not "unsafe"
All five adverse findings are **substandard under s.3(1)(zx)** of the FSS Act — the reports
say so in terms. That is the quality tier, addressed by **civil adjudication under s.51**,
not the **unsafe food** tier prosecuted under s.59. Keeping that distinction sharp is
probably the single most important framing point in the whole matter.

## 2. The strongest technical point available to you

**All three substandard defects are of manufacturing origin. None is a handling, storage or
transport defect.**

| Sample | Defect found | Where such a defect is created |
|---|---|---|
| Besan, Tulsi Gold (940230) | admixture of **wheat and pea starches** | **Milling / manufacture.** A distributor cannot introduce foreign starch into sealed flour. |
| Dahi, Samooh brand (940231) | milk fat **0.53%** against 4.5% | **Manufacture.** Fat is removed by separating the milk before setting. No handling failure lowers fat content. |
| Refined soyabean oil (940232) | **saponification value 196.8** (limit 195) | **Refining / blending.** Saponification value reflects mean fatty-acid chain length — a compositional property of the oil as made. |

This is worth stating precisely, because it is falsifiable and it holds. If these were
**handling** failures, the reports would have failed on the handling markers — peroxide
value, rancidity, moisture ingress, insect or rodent contamination, microbial growth. They
did not. Peroxide values were within limits, rancidity negative, and the besan was clear of
rodent hair, excreta and insects.

The saponification point deserves emphasis. That sample was recorded as **"Intact and
Unbroken"** — a sealed tin. **Degradation cannot explain an out-of-range saponification value
in oil that has never been heated.** In sealed stock, that figure points to the oil not being
what it was sold as. Whoever refined or blended it is upstream of whoever delivered it.

The practical consequence: on all three articles the chain of causation runs to the
**manufacturer or packer**, and the question for you becomes documentary — can you show what
you received, from whom, and that you passed it on unaltered.

## 4. The scope question that decides which rules apply to you

Are you a **goods supplier** or a **participant in food preparation**?

Your licence reads *Food Services – Caterer* and includes category 16 Prepared Foods, so on
its face you are licensed to cater, not merely to trade. The FIR's word **सहयोगी**
("associate") is vague and could mean sub-contractor, joint operator, or simply vendor.

- **If you supplied sealed packaged goods only** — the analysis is manufacturer/packer
  liability under s.27, and your defence is documentary traceability.
- **If your people cooked, handled or ran any part of the kitchen** — hygiene, storage and
  preparation become live against you directly, and the position is materially different.

Pin this down from your contract, purchase orders and delivery challans **before** you make
any representation. Do not characterise your role loosely in correspondence.

## 6. Independent testing — worth doing, with honest limits

If you hold retained stock of the same batches, test it at an **NABL-accredited**
laboratory, with documented chain of custody.

What it can do: same-batch results going to whether the defect was present as manufactured
are genuinely probative on the manufacturing-origin argument in section 2.

What it cannot do: it cannot rebut the official certificates. Those samples are destroyed,
and your private test is a different sample.

If oil is involved, **specifically instruct Total Polar Compounds** alongside the standard
panel. TPC is the one enforceable limit for used frying oil, it was never run on the official
kadhai sample, and its absence is the largest single gap in the laboratory evidence.

## 7. Statutory routes to raise with counsel

Confirm each provision before relying on it.

- **s.3(1)(zx) vs s.3(1)(zz)** — substandard versus unsafe. Insist on the distinction in
  every filing.
- **s.51** — penalty for substandard food; civil adjudication before an Adjudicating Officer.
- **s.59** — punishment for unsafe food. Requires the article to be unsafe; the reports do
  not establish it.
- **s.26** — responsibilities of the food business operator, including that a supplier is
  expected to sell food that complies.
- **s.27** — liability of manufacturers, packers, wholesalers, distributors and sellers. This
  is where the manufacturer-origin argument lives.
- **The referral-laboratory route** — re-analysis of a counterpart part of the sample. It is
  **time-bound**, so raise it immediately. Note the standing point: that right ordinarily
  belongs to the person from whom the sample was lifted, i.e. Shalu Associates or the school,
  not necessarily an upstream supplier. Your counsel may need to support their application
  rather than file one, or seek it if you are later arrayed.
- **s.28 recall** — see section 8.
- **BNS 274 / 275** — adulteration and sale of noxious food. Both require proof the
  laboratory work does not supply.

## 8. If you did supply any of the three — the part that is not about defending yourself

If a batch of "Tulsi Gold" besan is an admixture of wheat and pea starch, or a batch of
"Samooh" dahi contains 0.53% milk fat, then **those batches went to other customers too.**

That triggers a genuine obligation, not just a tactical question. Under the recall provisions
(s.28 and the FSSAI recall regulations) an FBO who learns that food it distributed is
non-compliant is expected to act. Concretely:

1. Identify every other consignment from the same batches and where it went.
2. Notify the manufacturer in writing and demand batch records.
3. Notify the Designated Officer and initiate withdrawal of the affected batches.
4. Stop further supply from those batches immediately.

Doing this promptly and on the record is also the strongest possible demonstration of good
faith, and mitigating. Failing to do it, having been put on notice by these certificates, is
far more serious than the original supply.

## 12. The three arguments that matter most

1. **Substandard is not unsafe.** All five findings are s.3(1)(zx) quality failures. BNS
   274/275 and FSS s.59 need more, and the evidence does not provide it.
2. **Every defect found is of manufacturing origin.** Foreign starch in besan, missing fat
   in dahi, and an off-specification saponification value in a sealed tin are all created
   before delivery. No handling marker failed on any sample.
3. **Causation was never established and now cannot be.** No microbiology, no water sample,
   every sample destroyed. Nothing links any supplied article to 49 children falling ill.

Argument 2 is yours to prove, and it is proved with invoices, batch numbers and signed
challans — not with argument.
