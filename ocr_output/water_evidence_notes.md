## 2. The three things this report cannot do

### 2.1 It contains no microbiology at all — this is decisive
The report is headed *Chemical Analysis of Water* and that is exactly what it is. There is
**no total coliform count, no *E. coli*, no faecal coliform** determination anywhere on it.

IS 10500:2012 requires total coliforms and *E. coli* to be **absent in 100 ml**. Those are
the parameters that matter in a suspected waterborne outbreak. Chemically unremarkable water
can be heavily contaminated microbiologically — the two are independent.

So the word **"Satisfactory"** in the remarks column means *satisfactory on the chemical and
physical parameters tested*. It is not a clearance of the water as safe to drink, and it must
not be presented as one. Equally, nobody can use it to exclude the water as the cause.

### 2.2 It was sampled two days after the children fell ill
Illness: **16/07/2026.** Sampling: **18/07/2026.** Water quality at a school tank or borewell
can change completely in 48 hours, especially if anyone flushed, cleaned or dosed the system
after the incident. This report describes the water on 18 July. It cannot describe the water
the children drank on 16 July.

Note also that the FSDA lifted eight **food** samples on 16/07 and **no water sample at all**.
The first water sample in this record is yours, two days late.

### 2.3 It is incompletely authenticated
Only the Chemist has signed. The Junior Engineer and Assistant Engineer blocks are empty, and
the Code No. and Block fields are blank. Obtain a **fully signed certified copy** from the
issuing office before relying on it anywhere.

## 3. The residual chlorine cell — unresolved, and it matters either way

On the copy supplied, a handwritten **"Nil"** sits between row 8 (sulphate) and row 9
(residual chlorine), and it cannot be assigned to a row with confidence. Row 10
(conductivity) carries a dash. **Get a certified copy and settle this**, because both
readings are significant and neither supports a bare "satisfactory":

**If "Nil" belongs to residual chlorine** — the water carried **no disinfectant residual** on
18/07. Against the form's own acceptable figure of 0.2 mg/l, that is a **deficiency, not a
pass**. No chlorine residual means no disinfection barrier at the point of use, which is
precisely the condition in which a microbiological contamination event becomes possible. On
this reading the report quietly supports the waterborne hypothesis rather than defeating it.

**If "Nil" belongs to sulphate** — then **residual chlorine was not determined at all**, and
the single most diagnostic parameter on the form is simply missing.

Either way, the conclusion for your purposes is the same: **this report does not establish
that the water was safe.**

## 4. The "14 chlorine" account — take it seriously, but not as forgery

A reported residual chlorine of ~14 mg/l would be roughly 35–70× the acceptable range on this
very form (0.2–0.4). Water at that level is strongly odorous and acutely irritant, and
ingestion can cause nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain — the presentation described in the
FIR. So the figure, if real, is not trivial and should be pursued.

But there is an explanation far more probable than record tampering, and far more useful:

> **Shock chlorination after the event.** Superchlorinating tanks and lines is the standard
> reflex response once a school suspects its water. If the system was dosed on or just after
> 16/07, a sample taken then could legitimately read very high, and free chlorine decays
> quickly — by 18/07 it could genuinely be nil. **"14 then nil" needs no forgery to explain.**

This reframing is much stronger for you than an allegation of tampering, for three reasons:

1. It is chemically ordinary and therefore credible.
2. It is **evidence that the school itself suspected the water** — otherwise why dose it.
3. It is provable from records that exist: dosing registers, bleaching-powder or hypochlorite
   purchase and issue records, tank cleaning logs, and the dates on them.

**Everything turns on the timing.** If a high chlorine reading predates the illness, it is a
candidate cause. If it postdates the illness, it is remediation — and still probative,
because it shows contemporaneous suspicion of the water. Establish the date and time before
building any argument on it.

## 5. On the belief that the school's records were altered

Handle this with discipline. At present the position is: your father heard a figure, and later
heard a different account. That is **hearsay, not evidence**, and conviction is not a
substitute for a document.

Three cautions, meant practically rather than morally:

- **An unsupported allegation that a District Magistrate ordered records falsified will
  destroy your credibility** on everything else you say, including the parts that are well
  founded. Once you are the party who made a wild allegation, your traceability documents get
  read in that light.
- It exposes you to **defamation and counter-proceedings**, which is a second front you do not
  need.
- It is unnecessary. The innocent explanations — different sampling points, different dates,
  two different laboratories, chlorine decay, post-incident dosing — account for the same
  facts, and the lawful routes below will surface a genuine discrepancy if one exists.

**Never allege alteration until you hold two inconsistent documents in your hand.** Then it is
no longer an allegation; it is a comparison, and it makes itself.

## 7. The sunlight-degraded bottle — do not test it, do preserve it

Be blunt with yourself about this one. A water sample stored **in direct sunlight for days**
is not a sample any more:

- **Chlorine** is gone. Free chlorine photolyses and off-gasses within hours. A nil result
  proves nothing about the source.
- **Microbiology** is meaningless. Coliforms die off, other organisms multiply, and the count
  no longer bears any relationship to the water as supplied. A result could be falsely clean
  *or* falsely alarming.
- **Container** matters. Microbiological sampling requires a sterile bottle, with sodium
  thiosulphate if the water is chlorinated. An ordinary bottle invalidates the test regardless
  of storage.
- Valid practice is transport at ~4 °C in the dark and analysis **within 6 hours**, 24 hours at
  the outside.

So: **do not send it for testing hoping it will help.** A clean result carries no weight and a
dirty result will be correctly attacked as an artefact of storage — and having commissioned it,
you own the result either way.

**Do keep it.** Do not destroy anything. Label it, photograph it, record where it has been
stored, and leave it sealed. Its value is as a physical exhibit in a custody narrative, not as
an analyte.

## 8. Fresh campus sampling — worth doing, done properly

You can still sample the live supply, and you should. Be clear about what it proves.

**It cannot** establish the water quality on 16 July 2026.

**It can** establish whether the supply is *currently* unchlorinated or contaminated. If it
is, that demonstrates a **systemic, continuing condition** rather than a one-off — which is
circumstantially relevant to July, and is in any case a live public-health matter, since
roughly 700 children are still drinking it.

If you do it, do it so that it cannot be dismissed as self-serving:

**Who** — through counsel. Best of all, request that the **Food Safety Officer, the Jal Nigam
engineer or the CMO's office draw the samples jointly**, or at minimum give written notice that
you are sampling and invite them to attend. A sample drawn by an interested party alone carries
little weight — which is already a weakness of C 720, collected by Shri Subhash Gupta, who is
named in the FIR narrative.

**Where** — sample every point separately, do not composite:
- the source (borewell / handpump / mains inlet)
- the underground sump
- the overhead tank
- the **kitchen tap used for cooking**
- the children's **drinking water points**
- the RO outlet installed by Shalu Associates after the incident

**What** — instruct explicitly:
- **Total coliforms and *E. coli*** (absent in 100 ml)
- **Residual chlorine**, measured **on site at the moment of collection**, not at the lab
- Turbidity, pH, TDS, nitrate, iron
- Full IS 10500:2012 panel if budget allows

**How** — NABL-accredited laboratory. Sterile bottles supplied by that laboratory. Sodium
thiosulphate for chlorinated points. Ice box at 4 °C, dark. Delivered within 6 hours.
Written chain-of-custody form signed at each handover. Photograph and video each collection
with the date and time visible, and record GPS if you can.

**Also record on site**, at each point, on the same day: residual chlorine by field kit, and
the condition of the tanks — last cleaning date, cover integrity, standing water, cross
connections, proximity of any soak pit or drain to the borewell.

## 9. What all of this actually does for Jay Ambey

You have now clarified that you supply **raw material** to Shalu Associates. That matters, and
it separates your two defences cleanly. Keep them separate, because they are independently
sufficient and they have different owners.

**Defence A — yours alone, and the stronger one.** The three substandard articles are
manufacturer-sealed branded goods, and every defect found is of **manufacturing origin**:
foreign starch in besan is introduced at milling, missing milk fat in dahi requires the milk
to be separated before setting, and an out-of-range saponification value cannot arise by
degradation in a tin recorded "Intact and Unbroken." No handling marker failed on any sample —
peroxide within limits, rancidity negative, no rodent hair, excreta or insects. This defence
rests on invoices, batch numbers and signed challans, and it does not depend on the water at
all. See [`supplier_position_notes.md`](supplier_position_notes.md).

**Defence B — shared, and weaker for you.** Causation was never established: no microbiology
on any food sample, no water sample on the day, every food sample destroyed. Water remains at
least as plausible a cause as anything supplied.

Two cautions on B:

1. **It is not really your argument.** "It was the water" primarily helps you by exculpating
   the *food*, but the water is the school's and the housekeeping contractor's responsibility,
   not Shalu Associates' either. Pressing it hard puts you in conflict with the institution
   your customer serves. Let counsel decide how far to push it and in what forum.
2. **It does not answer Defence A's question.** Even if the water were proven to be the cause,
   a substandard besan or dahi you supplied remains a separate s.51 quality matter. Do not let
   the water narrative distract from assembling the traceability pack, which is the thing that
   actually resolves your own exposure.
